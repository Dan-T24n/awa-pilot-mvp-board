0. Context and vocabulary

AWA’s authoritative pilot-MVP decision record states choices, reasons, and open questions. Specifications, user stories, and prospect material derive from it; a conflicting specification is wrong, and an abandoned decision must be changed here. Numbering does not make decisions optional. This record states behavior, not a delivery plan.

Pilot one is confirmed as of 2026-08-03: Tasco, in Tax/Accounting/Finance; pilots two and three are not. Each choice must work regardless of customer. Tasco’s operating entity, workflow, and sponsor plus dedicated owner remain unnamed and block start. AWA captures recurring-work know-how inside the customer’s governed material and keeps it theirs. It is not a data-residency product. Retrieval is the lower bar; captured, improving methods are the objective. Retrieval without method capture is only a search box.

The MVP is a rebuild. The existing FastAPI application remains the sales-demo surface but neither constrains nor credits it. Its repository is too disordered to govern delivery, and the demo showed an undiagnosed roughly seventy-second pause while application and database were awake. Those facts reject that foundation, not a governed, concurrent, multi-user system.

A pilot is one entity, recurring workflow, and dedicated owner—the smallest thing yielding a real decision, not a group rollout. Interviews, provisioning, sandbox setup, corpus loading, ingestion, and permission scoping are pre-pilot; the clock starts with real work. Demo and pilot use one product, never a customer-specific fork; pilot requests set the roadmap. Evidence is user behavior and feedback, not screenshots, tests, or delivery counts. At most three early pilots run at once—two in Vietnam and ideally one elsewhere in Southeast Asia—because the three-person team directly engages each.

A pilot requires all four binary entry conditions: named people who already do the work; their real documents, even if curated read-only; a job they were already accountable to do that week; and an agreed feedback channel and cadence that informs product change. A dedicated owner is the named customer interface able to convene users; the pilot cannot start without one, though the product does not model their internal authority. The fixed end date culminates in an expand, revise, or stop decision, preventing indefinite evaluation. A baseline of three to five completed real examples is reviewed before start for qualitative comparison, not a saving percentage or benchmark. Each of the three one-week iterations comprises real use, feedback, and a shipped change.

An Artifact is a user-owned document from chat or a Flow, then directly edited, shared, exported, and delivered. A Flow is a saved method from a chat that already worked; it re-retrieves against the invoker’s access in the background and produces an Artifact. Every Flow displays Gather, Reason, Produce, Check; phase instructions are stable while sub-actions vary and are display-only. Personal space is owner-only and unshareable, not an Artifact store. Outward delivery means AWA renders and records export, then the user delivers to a non-user. Citations retain markers and printed locators because recipients lack accounts. For pilots one through three, AWA proposes each classification and owning department; the customer approves before sign-in.

1. Pilot contract and acceptance

D1–D2. The pilot is a fixed three-week, three-iteration window, with the exit decision fixed before start and a mutable product inside it. Each week uses the product, gathers feedback, and ships a change; three cycles let a week-one change be assessed in week three. Day one requires non-disruptive deployment, no lost data, forced re-login, or broken in-flight Artifact or Flow, and work that survives schema changes. The baseline precedes the clock. Artifact, Flow, and citation contracts remain frozen even when presentation and quality improve.

The chosen workflow must run weekly or more often: a three-week pilot observes three weekly cycles and zero completed monthly or quarterly cycles. The previously used quarterly revenue snapshot and variance summary cannot be pilot workflows. Candidate qualification must ask cadence early. At Tasco, workflow choice is a live risk because monthly close and quarterly filing do not qualify; cash position, AR/AP aging review, and payment run are weekly candidates to test first. If the department has no weekly work, change the workflow or first cohort, not the window.

D3. The cohort is one executive over at least two peer units, each with a manager and workers. All produce real work. Executives see activity and shared or exported Flows and Artifacts across the cohort; managers see the same in their unit; neither sees private drafts or personal uploads. Two peer units are the minimum because one unit never tests differing perimeters. The hierarchy tests the governance-chain claim, creates real output readers, and lets the executive make the exit decision from firsthand evidence. Tasco’s three teams are the peer units and product departments; the intended cohort is about nine or ten people: one executive, plus a manager and two or three workers per team. Onboarding fixes the roster. The accepted cost is hand-onboarding users and documents and interviewing every producer weekly—roughly five hours plus synthesis for the three-person team.

D4. Primary exit evidence is binary task completion: whether each producer completed their genuine recurring job in each cycle. Nine or ten people over three cycles yield 27–30 observations. Quality is the effort-to-export trend: manual editing and rerunning between generation and sending should decline after the user teaches a Flow. Heavy cycle-one editing is expected; cycle-three decline demonstrates compounding. A rerun after an instruction is learning; an unchanged rerun is failure. Editing wrong output is failure; editing to add irreplaceable human judgment is correct behavior. Export, counted per completed cycle rather than raw volume, is the satisfaction signal: voluntarily putting one’s name on an Artifact and sending it outside the cohort.

A wrong disclosure is investigated immediately and carried into the exit decision, not automatically stopped; it outranks other engineering priorities. AWA-proposed, customer-approved misclassification differs from enforcement failure; only the latter evidences authorization behavior. Users need a reporting channel, since zero reports otherwise prove nothing. No time-saving percentage is claimed: mutable-product timing is weak, and the baseline is qualitative only. The MVP records generated/exported content for diffing; generation, edit, and export times; Flow version and instruction changes; export distinct from sharing; and user, Artifact version, time, and source classifications.

D5. The core loop must work first: ask in chat, receive a cited answer, promote it to an Artifact, edit it, and export it. It supports the weekly job on day one and includes non-negotiable retrieval governed by user level/department against document classification/owning department. The compounding loop saves a successful chat as a Flow, runs it in the background, attaches corrective instructions, and improves later runs. It must work at least partially. Expected evidence is core-loop-heavy cycle one, Flow creation around cycle two, and less editing in cycle three. Build core before compounding.

2. Governance and runtime foundations

D6–D7. Before the clock starts, AWA interviews users, collects/ingests the corpus, proposes document classifications and owning departments, and obtains customer approval of the per-document list. This avoids customer-authored-policy delay, reveals needed classification rules, and needs no customer-facing administration console. Approval attributes a later disclosure to approved scoping or enforcement, at an accepted onboarding cost. Customer-authored classification remains a product differentiator but is not live in pilots one through three, where AWA authors proposals; do not present this as the permanent model or a live customer-authorship claim. Manual operation does not scale beyond pilot three, and that is accepted.

Users may upload only into personal space. Personal documents are retrievable solely by their owner, never enter the shared perimeter or administrator path, and never require user classification. They cannot be shared; a user may instead share a derived Artifact with named people. Native-text PDF, DOCX, XLSX, TXT, and Markdown are required. OCR of scanned documents is excluded; scans are identified during onboarding. The stated gap is ingesting Vietnamese paperwork, not understanding Vietnamese.

D8. Each pilot customer receives one deployment and one database; up to three pilots run three instances of the same image. Infrastructure, rather than code, is the isolation boundary. It provides a short security answer, makes per-customer location literal, and removes query-level organization scoping, cross-tenant leak testing, and tenant-aware caching. The organization identifier stays because late tenancy is expensive and a customer with two entities in one deployment will need it. Deployment must reach every live instance without disrupting users. Shared product means same image and interface, not shared deployment. One entity per deployment limits evidence to department and level governance, not group-wide multi-entity governance. Tasco’s operating entity remains unnamed. Host, database, model provider, and storage are open, but any substrate must provide separate instance/database, deploy-resilient sessions, deployment to all live instances, and named region per customer.

D9–D10. Oversight sees who ran what and how often, shared or exported Artifacts and Flows, and access/export records. Managers are limited to their department; executives are organization-wide. Unshared drafts and personal uploads remain owner-only, and users are told this on day one. This makes personal space credible and preserves unobserved experimentation.

Customer-facing roles are worker, manager, executive; AWA operator is backstage. Users have department and level; role changes only oversight reach and ability to read restricted material. Documents have classification and owning department. Public is for every authenticated user; Internal for every level in its owning department; Confidential for managers in its owning department; Restricted for executives organization-wide; Personal for its owner alone, never shareable. Levels accumulate upward. Executive access overrides departmental scope except for another person’s personal material, so executive oversight can be organization-wide. No per-user exception or escape hatch exists. A needed document outside the matrix requires reconsidering its classification through AWA-operated, customer-approved scoping. Reclassification widens access for everyone it reaches, an accepted blunter cost. Restricted answers the board-material question but is untested with one executive. At Tasco, “department” means each of three teams, not the larger Tax/Accounting/Finance department. Managers grant visibility only, never Flow approval.

D11–D12. Authentication is work-email magic link, accelerated by Google sign-in for Workspace customers; both resolve verified email to one account. There are no passwords. Sliding sessions extend with use, survive deploys, last through normal weekly work while remaining defensible to security review, and offer sign-out. Admission requires the customer domain and a per-customer provisioned-address allowlist. AWA can hand-generate a link if mail filters block delivery; exact session duration is a specification setting.

The interface toggles English and Vietnamese, retrieval works over Vietnamese corpora, and answers use the question’s language at an onward-sendable standard. Vietnamese-capable chunking and embeddings are needed because English-tuned defaults may quietly fail on diacritics and segmentation. Model choice is more constrained by Vietnamese output quality than English. These tests belong to substrate selection.

3. Core work loop — from governed answer to outward delivery

D13–D14. Outward delivery is MVP scope. AWA renders a finished Artifact as a downloadable file; the user delivers it. AWA sends no mail and holds neither address book nor recipient address. This enables the real external outcome without operating as a customer-domain sender. Export deliberately bypasses the retrieval perimeter: a user may carry legitimately retrieved classified material out; no classification block is proposed because it would break the job. AWA records exporting user, Artifact and exact version, time, and source classification levels, both for security review and as D4 evidence. PDF and DOCX are always available; XLSX is available only for Artifacts containing a table. PPTX is excluded and iteration-one requests are ranked with other feedback.

D15–D19. Artifacts are directly editable documents, not suggestion-approval mechanisms, and can be shared for comment. Editing must keep users out of Word, where citations, versions, export, and provenance would be lost. Content uses prose and table blocks; generated figures and charts are out. The Knowledge Base is a flat searchable, filterable reader, not a folder hierarchy. Citations are the main entry point and search supports verification or finding a file; a tree requires the filing knowledge the product seeks to remove and fails at scale.

Artifacts share only with named people, making disclosure of confidential-derived content deliberate and attributable. This is knowingly inadequate for a future forty-person reference document but is post-pilot. Each material claim has an inline citation opening its supporting page or sheet. Exports preserve markers and list document title and locator, then Artifact owner, version, and export date. Locators are pages for PDF, DOCX, TXT, and Markdown and sheets for XLSX. Passage-level highlighting is excluded because durable offsets and cross-format viewers cost more than needed.

The rebuild retains the observed demo shape: landing opens chat, not a dashboard; the rail shows name, department, and authorized-document count; Knowledge Base, Artifacts, and Flows sit beside chats; and a successful chat can create a Flow. It changes document source chips to inline locator citations and free-form run steps to the fixed phase spine. This avoids redesign cost and preserves the pilot’s focus on completion, effort-to-export, and disclosures.

4. Shared Artifact and Flow contracts

D20–D21. Artifacts and Flows use named-person sharing. Owners grant view or edit; Flow view includes run. Everyone with access may comment, copy, or fork; only owners delete, reshare, or transfer. Per-recipient rights are familiar; comment cannot be withheld because review is feedback, and copy prevention is theatre. A Flow edit can change others’ results, so each run records its version and changes notify everyone with access.

Objects autosave. Before first share or export they are unversioned owner-only drafts; from first publication, every saved change creates a version and shares, exports, and runs record it. There is no save button. This preserves private drafts, gives recipients stable referents, supports Flow tracing and trust signals, and stamps exact Artifact versions on exports and rendered citations.

5. Compounding loop — capture, run, and improve a method

D22–D25. A Flow has no approval gate. Its creator owns and changes it; review means sharing and invited comment. Requiring sign-off would recreate the delay AWA rejects. Trust comes instead from owner and department, run count, last successful run, and last-edit time.

Flows are durable background jobs. Users may run multiple Flows and chats concurrently, including simultaneous chats for one user; runs survive navigation or tab closure, cannot exhaust the instance, and record state, time, version, and outcome. Each run resolves sources against the invoker’s current access at run time—not the author’s access or a cached snapshot.

Users correct a Flow rather than author a graph, DAG, or opaque prompt. All runs display Gather, Reason, Produce, Check. Sub-actions vary and explain the run, but only phases receive instructions. The fixed spine is a testable agent-harness hypothesis: build the thinnest version that can be disproved and harden it only after three cycles. It solves the demonstrated unstable, repeated current-build step traces and gives correction targets that remain stable across runs.

D26–D28. Soft knowledge is seven concrete types: source preference and exclusions in Gather; thresholds and audience anticipation in Reason; output shape plus register and vocabulary in Produce; provenance habits in Check. This mapping is a proposed test, not proof. Gather stores retrieval intent and source rules, never fixed identifiers; each run re-retrieves and records actual documents for currency and auditability.

Instructions arrive when a user writes one on a phase or when the system proposes a candidate rule from Artifact corrections. One correction is enough to propose from because the pilot allows at most three weekly runs, but no candidate is silently adopted: its owner confirms or dismisses it in one tap. A Flow is created only from a chat that already worked, never from an empty form. It uses a plain-text editor with up to four editable phase instructions and no whole-Flow instruction blob.

D29–D30. Runs use chat’s existing rendering and completion notification; there is no Activity page. A run belongs to a Flow and produces an Artifact, and anything needed tomorrow belongs in Artifacts. A shared Flow shows owner, department, run count, last successful run, and last-edit time, not an endorsement badge. A new Flow has no run signal, an accepted cost for a colocated cohort of about ten; badges are excluded because even non-gating badges imply approval.

6. Out of the MVP

The MVP excludes user-authored workflow structure (step/DAG builders, branching, conditionals); autonomous scheduled or event-triggered Flow execution; Flow chaining; approval gates; simultaneous co-editing; Flow search, categories, and marketplace; PPTX, generated figures/charts, and OCR; customer-facing administration; application-level cross-tenant query scoping; passage-level citation highlighting; broad sharing and sharing personal source documents; and per-user clearance grants. In-scope alternatives are user-started durable background runs, a simple four-to-six-Flow list, page/sheet citations, named sharing, and matrix-based access.

7. Open questions

These six questions do not reopen the record. Questions 1–3 must be answered before a pilot starts and are live for pilot one; question 4 before substantial platform work; question 5 before the phase-spine contract enters a specification; and question 6 before the instruction-attachment contract enters a specification.

  1. How will weekly feedback be collected, and by whom?
  2. What happens to customer data when the pilot ends?
  3. Who supports users when something breaks mid-pilot?
  4. Which host, database, model provider, and storage will be used? The demo uses Fly.io in Singapore and Neon Postgres; AWS with Bedrock, a VPC, and S3-style storage has been discussed, but neither is committed. The choice must preserve D8’s isolation, deploy, session, and named-region properties and D12’s Vietnamese requirements.
  5. Is Check necessary? Only provenance habits currently use it and may be a Produce rule. Test Gather, Reason, and Produce before hardening the spine.
  6. Is phase-level instruction attachment sufficient, or must instructions attach to sub-actions? Phase-level is the working assumption because prose can be arbitrarily specific, but it remains testable rather than hardened.

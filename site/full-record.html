0. Context and vocabulary

0.1 Context

This record is AWA’s authority for the pilot MVP: its choices, reasons, and open questions. Specifications, user stories, and prospect material derive from it; a conflicting specification is wrong, and an abandoned decision must be changed here. It records product behaviour, not a delivery plan.

Pilot one is confirmed as of 2026-08-03: Tasco’s Tax/Accounting/Finance department. The operating entity, weekly-or-faster workflow, sponsor, and dedicated owner remain unnamed and block the start. Pilots two and three are not confirmed. The pilot tests recurring real work, not a customer-specific fork; it ends in expand, revise, or stop.

0.2 Vocabulary

An Artifact is an editable document created from chat or a Flow, then shared, exported, and delivered. A Flow saves a chat that already worked, re-retrieves material against the invoker’s current access, and produces an Artifact. Personal is owner-only and unshareable. Outward delivery means AWA renders and records an export; the user sends it outside AWA.

1. Pilot contract and acceptance

D1 — Fixed pilot window and live iteration

Decision: Run each pilot for a fixed three-week window with three one-week iterations and a pre-agreed final expand, revise, or stop decision; keep the product mutable within that window. Each week combines real use, feedback, and a shipped change. Deployments must preserve sessions and in-flight Artifacts and Flows, and work created in week one must survive schema changes into week three. The contracts for Artifacts, Flows, and citations stay fixed even while their usability improves; the baseline is captured before the clock starts.

D2 — Weekly-or-faster pilot workflow

Decision: Select only a recurring workflow that occurs at least weekly. A three-week pilot can observe three weekly cycles but no complete monthly or quarterly cycle, so quarterly revenue snapshots and variance summaries cannot qualify. For Tasco's Tax/Accounting/Finance pilot, the still-blocking workflow must be weekly or more frequent—cash position, AR/AP aging review, or payment run are candidates. If no such workflow exists, change the workflow or first cohort, not the pilot duration.

D3 — Hierarchical cohort across peer units

Decision: Pilot one executive over at least two peer units, each with a manager and workers; all participants do real work. Managers see activity plus shared or exported Artifacts and Flows in their unit, while the executive sees that information across the cohort, never private drafts or personal uploads. Tasco's three teams form the product's peer units; the intended cohort is about nine or ten people, with exact roster and executive set in pre-pilot onboarding. Two peer units are the minimum because differing perimeters must be observed, not merely configured.

D4 — Evidence for the exit decision

Decision: Base the final decision primarily on per-person, per-cycle task completion, with effort from generation to outward delivery read as a three-cycle trend and disclosure incidents investigated immediately. Count voluntary exports per completed cycle as the satisfaction signal. Separate AWA-proposed, customer-approved scoping mistakes from authorization-enforcement failures; only the latter evidences product authorization. Distinguish a rerun after an instruction from a no-change rerun, and judgment-added edits from wrong-output edits. Do not claim a time-saving percentage: the baseline supports qualitative comparison, not a business case.

D5 — Core loop before compounding loop

Decision: Build and prove the core work loop first: governed chat answer, cited result, Artifact promotion, editing, and export. The compounding loop—save successful work as a Flow, run it, attach corrections, and improve subsequent runs—must work at least partially during the window. Permission-governed retrieval is day-one core-loop scope, not a later feature. A user cannot create a Flow until chat has already produced acceptable work, so a weak first cycle prevents the learning curve of declining editing and rerunning that the pilot needs to observe.

2. Governance and runtime foundations

D6 — AWA-operated onboarding and scoping

Decision: AWA interviews users, collects and ingests the corpus, proposes each document's classification and owning department, and obtains customer approval of the per-document list before first sign-in. There is no customer-facing administrator console in the pilot. This removes customer-authored policy as an onboarding blocker and makes a reported disclosure traceable either to approved scoping or enforcement. It is deliberately unscalable beyond pilot three, and customer authorship remains a product differentiator that is not yet demonstrated in these pilots.

D7 — Owner-only personal uploads

Decision: Permit uploads only into an owner-only Personal space; users never classify those documents and cannot place them in the shared perimeter. A personal document is unshareable by every role, including executives, though an Artifact derived from it can be shared with named people under the Artifact rules. Support native-text PDF, DOCX, XLSX, TXT, and Markdown. Exclude OCR: scanned documents are identified and excluded during pre-pilot onboarding, so the MVP does not misrepresent Vietnamese paperwork ingestion as a language-understanding issue.

D8 — Separate deployment and database per customer

Decision: Operate one deployment and one database for each pilot customer, with up to three instances of the same product image. Infrastructure—not application query logic—is the isolation boundary, avoiding pilot requirements for cross-tenant scoping, cache isolation, and cross-tenant leak tests. Retain an organization identifier for future multi-entity needs. The operating entity within Tasco remains unnamed and blocks the pilot deployment boundary; one entity per deployment means the pilot does not evidence a group-wide, multi-entity claim. Any substrate must support named regional location, deploys to all live instances, and session continuity.

D9 — Bounded oversight, disclosed on day one

Decision: Give managers and executives visibility into activity, shared or exported Artifacts and Flows, and access/export records at their permitted scopes, but never into unshared drafts or personal uploads. Tell users this boundary explicitly on day one during onboarding. Oversight supplies the sponsor with evidence of use, output quality, and disclosures without turning unfinished work into surveillance. Preserving private experimentation is essential: otherwise users keep working files outside AWA and the pilot loses both the work and the learning evidence it was designed to capture.

D10 — Role-and-department entitlement matrix

Decision: Use three customer roles—worker, manager, executive—and a document matrix of public, internal, confidential, restricted, and personal classifications. Public is available to authenticated users; internal is available within its owning department; confidential requires a manager in that department; restricted is executive-only organization-wide; personal is owner-only and never shareable. Levels are cumulative within a department, and executives read every non-personal classification. “Department” means the customer's smallest owning unit, which is a Tasco team in pilot one. Do not add per-user exceptions; reclassify through customer-approved scoping instead.

D11 — Passwordless work-email authentication

Decision: Use a magic link sent to the user's work email, with one-click Google sign-in where the customer uses Google Workspace. Both authenticate the same email-keyed user record; passwords, password resets, and a separate identity model are excluded. Restrict access both to the customer domain and to provisioned addresses in that customer's isolated deployment. Use a sliding session that survives deploys and supports explicit sign-out. Mailbox access is recovery; AWA can hand-generate a link when corporate filtering prevents normal delivery, an acceptable manual fallback for a small cohort.

D12 — Vietnamese-capable product

Decision: Support English and Vietnamese interface modes, Vietnamese-corpus retrieval, and answers in the question's language at an onward-sendable quality. The initial pilots are in Vietnam, so English-only retrieval or output would fail the first real question, potentially without an obvious error. The chosen retrieval stack must handle Vietnamese segmentation and diacritics rather than silently relying on English-tuned defaults. Model choice is consequently judged more strictly on Vietnamese answer quality than English quality, because outward delivery measures whether users will put their names on the result.

3. Core work loop — from governed answer to outward delivery

D13 — File export for outward delivery

Decision: Keep outward delivery in pilot scope by rendering a finished Artifact as a downloadable file that the user sends independently. AWA neither sends email nor stores an address book or recipient address. Export intentionally lets a user take permitted content outside the in-product perimeter because blocking that would prevent the actual job; no classification-based export block is proposed. At MVP, record the exporting user, Artifact and exact version, time, and source classifications. Render citations in the file because external recipients have neither an AWA account nor a live session.

D14 — Content-aware export formats

Decision: Always offer PDF and DOCX, and offer XLSX only when the Artifact contains a table. The available format follows the content: narrative prose in a spreadsheet is a broken outcome, while tables legitimately need spreadsheet delivery. PPTX is outside the MVP. If a pilot requests PPTX in the first iteration, treat it as feedback to rank rather than an implicit scope expansion.

D15 — Editable, commentable Artifacts

Decision: Make the Artifact a directly editable document that colleagues can comment on, not a suggestion-and-approval layer. Artifacts use composed prose and table blocks rather than a single prose dump, preserving editing, citations, versions, and export inside AWA instead of forcing users into Word. Generated figures and charts are excluded from the MVP; a request for them can be ranked during the pilot. Direct editing is required because accountable office users will change AI output, including for judgments only they can supply.

D16 — Search-first Knowledge Base

Decision: Make the Knowledge Base a document reader with a flat searchable, filterable list, not a browsable folder hierarchy. Citations are the primary route into supporting material; search is for locating a file directly. A hierarchy assumes users already know the filing system that AWA is meant to overcome, and a corpus small enough to browse during onboarding would produce the wrong design for a much larger corpus. The reader must support verification of a cited page or sheet.

D17 — Named-person Artifact sharing

Decision: Share an Artifact only with named people, never a department or broad audience. An Artifact may contain content derived from confidential sources, and broad sharing would distribute it beyond the recipients who could retrieve those sources themselves without a deliberate, attributable decision. This is a governance rule rather than a simplification. The known limit is team-wide reference material at scale; it is a post-pilot issue with an obvious future upgrade path, not an MVP reason to weaken named sharing.

D18 — Inline, locator-aware citations

Decision: Place an inline marker beside every material claim and take the reader to its supporting page or sheet. In exported files, retain the marker and provide a source list with document title and printed locator, followed by Artifact owner, version, and export date. Use page locators for PDF, DOCX, TXT, and Markdown, and sheet locators for XLSX; do not require cell-level precision. Passage-level highlighting is excluded because it requires durable character offsets and cross-format viewer support. Locator-aware ingestion is an accepted MVP cost.

D19 — Preserve the proven interface shape

Decision: Rebuild the product underneath the current interface shape rather than redesign the shell. Chat remains the landing experience; the rail shows the user's name, department, and live authorized-document count; Knowledge Base, Artifacts, and Flows sit beside the chat list. The rebuild replaces document-level source chips with inline page-or-sheet citations and free-form run steps with the fixed phase spine. This preserves the prospect-facing surface already shown while concentrating pilot effort on task completion, delivery effort, and governance evidence rather than cosmetic redesign.

4. Shared Artifact and Flow contracts

D20 — One named-recipient sharing model

Decision: Apply one sharing model to Artifacts and Flows: owners select a named person and grant view or edit; Flow view includes run; commenting and copying are always available; only the owner may delete, reshare, or transfer ownership. Do not create a comment-only tier or pretend copying can be prevented when instructions are readable text. A Flow edit affects future runs, so every run records its version and changes notify people with access. One model halves the surface to build, document, and test.

D21 — Autosave, publication, and versioning

Decision: Autosave Artifacts and Flows continuously. Keep an unshared, unexported object as an unversioned owner-only draft; from first share or export onward, every saved change cuts a version. Every share, export, and Flow run records the version used, with no save button. Versions begin only when another person needs a stable referent, avoiding meaningless private-draft history. A changed shared Flow notifies people with access, and export records and rendered citations identify the precise Artifact version that left AWA.

5. Compounding loop — capture, run, and improve a method

D22 — No Flow approval gates

Decision: The creator owns and changes a Flow; no superior signs it off. Review means the owner shares the Flow and invites comment, while autosave and version records provide traceability. Approval would impose a queue on the people whose methods the product is designed to capture. Replace its implied trust signal with observable owner, department, run, success, and edit facts rather than a gate. Keep the distinction precise: users may review proposed instructions, but “review” never becomes a condition that blocks Flow use.

D23 — Durable, concurrent Flow runs

Decision: Treat Flows as durable background jobs and support multiple simultaneous Flows and chats, including multiple chats from the same user. A run must survive navigation away or tab closure, avoid allowing one user to exhaust the instance, and record state, time, executed version, and outcome. Crucially, each background run re-resolves sources against the invoker's access when it runs, never the Flow author's rights or a cached access snapshot. Concurrency is table stakes for office users, not an advanced feature.

D24 — Correct runs; do not author workflows

Decision: Do not ask users to build a graph or workflow or maintain an opaque saved prompt. The agent executes a Flow, renders its work as phases, and lets the user attach an instruction to a phase. Every Flow has the same fixed phase layer, while displayed sub-actions vary by run and remain explanatory rather than editable attachment points. This turns observed work into a precise correction surface without requiring office users to think like programmers or author an automation structure.

D25 — Fixed Gather–Reason–Produce–Check spine

Decision: Use Gather, Reason, Produce, and Check as the universal phase spine, while treating it as a pilot hypothesis rather than a hardened design. The agent may take variable sub-actions within a phase, but phases never move, giving instructions stable targets across runs. The spine answers the observed instability of free-form traces and fits common office work: find material, reason over it, produce an output, and check it. Build the thinnest testable version and test whether three phases would suffice before hardening the contract.

D26 — Seven categories of soft knowledge

Decision: Model soft knowledge as seven concrete categories: source preference, exclusions, thresholds, audience anticipation, output shape, register and vocabulary, and provenance habits. These can be viewed provisionally across Gather, Reason, Produce, and Check, but the placement is not proof that the phase spine is final. Gather retains retrieval intent and source rules rather than fixed document identifiers, so a Flow re-retrieves on each run. Record the documents actually used, preserving auditability as the corpus changes.

D27 — Explicit or confirmed correction-derived instructions

Decision: Let users add instructions directly to a phase or let the product propose a candidate instruction from a correction to the produced Artifact; never adopt the second path silently. One correction is enough to trigger a proposal because a weekly job provides at most three pilot runs, and owner confirmation or dismissal costs one tap. Silent learning would turn a one-off edit into a permanent rule. The Flow has no approval gate, but its owner must explicitly confirm any correction-derived instruction.

D28 — Create Flows from successful chats

Decision: Create a Flow only by saving a chat session that has already produced acceptable work; do not offer an empty Flow-authoring form. This makes Flow creation one action on a proven session plus a plain-text editor. A Flow holds up to four editable instruction texts, one for each phase, with no separate whole-Flow instruction blob. Both user-written instructions and system-proposed candidates therefore remain attached to the observed phase they amend, preserving the correction model without additional workflow-authoring surface.

D29 — No separate run destination

Decision: Do not create an Activity page or other standalone destination for running work. Render in-flight and recent Flow runs as users already see a chat answer, and notify users when a run completes. Every run belongs to a Flow and produces an Artifact; the run explains how work happened, while the Artifact is the enduring work. Anything users need tomorrow belongs in Artifacts, so treating runs as a peer navigation object would introduce workflow-engine thinking without a distinct user need.

D30 — Observable Flow trust signals

Decision: Show a shared Flow's owner and department, run count, last successful run, and last-edit time; provide no endorsement badge and no approval gate. These observable facts let a colleague assess whether reuse is worthwhile without waiting for authority. Accept that a new Flow with no runs has little signal; in a small colocated cohort, knowing the owner covers that gap. An optional badge would still read as approval even if it blocked nothing, undermining the deliberately no-approval Flow model.

Out of the MVP

The MVP excludes user-authored workflow structures such as DAGs, branches, and conditionals; autonomous schedules and event triggers; Flow-to-Flow composition; and any approval gate. It also excludes simultaneous co-editing, Flow discovery surfaces such as search, categories, or a marketplace, customer-facing administration, cross-tenant query scoping, and per-user clearance grants. Do not add PPTX export, generated figures or charts, OCR for scanned documents, passage-level citation highlighting, broad Artifact sharing, or any sharing of personal source documents. User-started durable background runs remain in scope; autonomous firing does not.

Open questions

  1. Feedback operation: Decide how weekly feedback is collected and who collects it before the pilot starts.
  2. End-of-pilot data handling: Decide what happens to customer data when the pilot ends before the pilot starts.
  3. Mid-pilot support: Decide who supports users when something breaks before the pilot starts.
  4. Platform substrate: Choose host, database, model provider, and storage before substantial platform work. Neither the current Fly.io/Singapore/Neon setup nor the discussed AWS/Bedrock/VPC/S3-style option is committed; the choice must preserve per-customer isolation, named region, deploy continuity, Vietnamese retrieval, and onward-sendable Vietnamese output.
  5. Phase-spine sufficiency: Test whether provenance habits belong in Produce rather than Check and whether Gather, Reason, and Produce can replace the four-phase spine before the contract enters a specification.
  6. Instruction granularity: Test whether phase-level instruction attachment is sufficient or must reach sub-actions. Phase-level attachment is the working assumption because prose can express arbitrary specificity, not a hardened contract.
